B S T R A C T
Complex molecular mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of Parkinson’s disease (PD) are gradually
being elucidated. Accumulating genetic evidence implicates dysfunction of kinase activities and
phosphorylation pathways in the pathogenesis of PD. Causative and risk gene products associated with
PD include protein kinases (such as PINK1, LRRK2 and GAK) and proteins related phosphorylation
signaling pathways (such as SNCA, DJ-1). PINK1, LRRK2 and several PD gene products have been
associated with mitogen-activated protein (MAP) and protein kinase B (AKT) kinase signaling pathways.
C-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERK) and p38, signaling pathways
downstream of MAP, are particularly important in PD. JNK and p38 play an integral role in neuronal
death. Targeting JNK or p38 signaling may offer an effective therapy for PD. Inhibitors of the ERK

1. Introduction
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a common neurodegenerative
disorder characterized by selective loss of dopaminergic neurons
in the substantia nigra and formation of intracellular inclusions
known as Lewy bodies in the remaining cells (Braak et al., 2004;
Greenamyre and Hastings, 2004). Through identification of the
genes involved in familial and sporadic PD during the past ten
years, the pathogenesis of PD has been related to impairments of
the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) (Cook and Petrucelli,
2009; Matsuda and Tanaka, 2010), the autophagy-lysosome
pathway (ALP) (Pan et al., 2008), mitochondrial metabolism
disturbance (Cali et al., 2011; Ho et al., 2012) and alternated
kinase activity (Cookson et al., 2007). Among these systems, the
roles of kinases and kinase pathways have become an increasing
focus of research and mutations in several genes encoding protein
kinases and proteins involved in phosphorylation signaling
pathways have been unequivocally linked to PD. Such protein
kinases include PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1) (Valente et al.,
2001, 2004), leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2) (Funayama
et al., 2002; Kumari and Tan, 2009) and Cyclin-G-associated
kinase (GAK) (Dumitriu et al., 2011; Rhodes et al., 2011). These
kinases, or their associated pathways, including mitogen-activat-
ed protein kinases (MAPK) such as extracellular signal-regulated
protein kinases (ERK), c-Jun N-t

2. Kinase genes associated with PD: Loci, physiological function
and pathological role
Protein kinases catalyze protein phosphorylation, which
usually results in functional changes in enzyme activity, cellular
location or association with other proteins (Fig. 1). The human
genome contains approximately 500 protein kinase genes, which
constitute about 2% of all human genes. Up to 30% of all human
proteins may be modified by kinase activity and kinases are known
to function as regulators of the majority of cellular pathways and as
switches in a number of cellular processes including proliferation,
differentiation, survival and death (Manning et al., 2002).

2.1. PINK1 (PARK6): Loci, physiological function and pathological role
PINK1 was first identified in tumor cell expression profile
experiments and localized to the PARK6 locus on chromosome
1p35–36. Because of the characteristics of transcriptional activa-
tion by PTEN, it was named PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)
(Valente et al., 2001, 2004). Among the 40 or more PINK1
mutations identified in symptomatic individuals, 15 segregate
with disease in pedigrees of early onset Parkinsonism (EOP).
Mutations in PINK1 have been discovered in different regions
including Europe, Asia and North America families with ARPD (Li
et al., 2005; Zimprich, 2011). PINK1 mutations have been identified
predominantly in familial cases, but interestingly, a higher
incidence of heterozygous PINK1 mutations occurs in sporadic
PD patients (Abou-Sleiman et al., 2006; Bonifati, 2012). Immuno-
histochemistry of PD brain sections demonstrated PINK1 immu-
noreactivity in a small proportion (5–10%) of neuronal inclusions
resembling Lewy bodies (Gandhi et al., 2006), supporting its role in
PD pathology.
2.1.1. Physiological function of PINK1
As a protein serine/threonine kinase, early findings indicate a
function for PINK1 in intracellular signaling pathways implicated
in PD pathogenesis. Three emerging properties of PINK1 are
especially relevant. First, primary sequence analysis and biochem-
ical studies have demonstrated that PINK1 is a member of the
eukaryotic protein kinase family of enzymes that are involved in
transduction and amplification of intracellular signals (Silvestri
et al., 2005; Sim et al., 2006). Protein phosphorylation is likely to
play an important role in PD pathogenesis and PINK1 may
participate by directly phosphorylating or binding key cellular
proteins governing neuronal survival. Second, the N-terminal
region of PINK1 contains a mitochondrial targeting motif. Initial

exogenous stress and genetic predisposition. The loss of PINK1
function causes mitochondrial dysfunction and increases cellular
susceptibility to stress. Prior studies have indicated that PINK1
phosphorylates the downstream effector TRAP1 and the down-
stream interactor Omi/HtrA2 (PARK13) and plays an essential role
in regulation of mitochondrial integrity in the PINK1 related
pathway (Abeliovich, 2007; Pridgeon et al., 2007; Yun et al., 2008).

3.2.4. Interaction between the PI3K/AKT and JNK signaling pathways
The PI3K/AKT and JNK pathways are the two most important
signaling cascades involved in PD, and cross-talk between these
two pathways determines the equilibrium between neuronal
death and survival. AKT negatively regulates the phosphorylation
and activation of JNK by directly phosphorylating apoptosis signal-
regulating kinase 1 (ASK1) and JNK-interacting protein 1 (JIP1),
thus preventing cell apoptosis and promoting cell survival (Kim
et al., 2001, 2002). Interestingly, in 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium
(MPP+)-induced human neuroblastoma SH-EP1 cells, IGF-1 poten-
tiated PI3K/AKT activity negatively regulates the JNK-related
apoptotic pathway. Furthermore, this negative regulation has
been shown to be mediated by AKT-dependent GSK3b inactivation
(Wang et al., 2010). Thus, the balance between activation of the
AKT pathway and inhibition of the JNK pathway could directly
determine cell fate (death or survival) in Parkinsonism settings.
