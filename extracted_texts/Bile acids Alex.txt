Abstract
46
47 Recent studies reveal that gut microbes produce diverse bile acid conjugates, termed
48 microbially conjugated bile acids (MCBAs). However, their regulation and health effects
49 remain unclear. Here, we analyzed early-life MCBA patterns and their link to islet
50 autoimmunity. We quantified 110 MCBAs in 303 stool samples collected longitudinally (3–
51 36 months) from children who developed one or more islet autoantibodies and controls who
52 remained autoantibody-negative. Stool MCBAs showed distinct age-dependent trajectories
53 and correlated with gut microbiome composition. Altered levels of ursodeoxycholic and
54 deoxycholic acid conjugates were linked to islet autoimmunity as well as modulated
55 monocyte activation in response to immunostimulatory lipopolysaccharide and Th17/Treg
56 cell balance. These findings suggest MCBAs influence immune development and type 1
57 diabetes risk.

Introduction
59
60 Bile acids (BAs) are cholesterol-derived surfactants produced from cholesterol in the liver,
61 stored in the gall bladder, and released into the small intestine upon meal consumption1-3. The
62 primary BAs produced in the hepatocytes consist of cholic acid (CA), chenodeoxycholic acid
63 (CDCA), and their amidates (i.e., either glycine or taurine conjugates)4. Gut microbes modify
64 these host-derived primary BAs to a broad range of secondary BAs via a series of reactions
65 including deconjugation, dehydroxylation, dehydrogenation, oxidation, and epimerization5.
66 Almost 95% of these BAs (both primary and secondary) are recirculated to the liver via the
67 enterohepatic circulation across the distal small intestine6. The remaining 5% still undergo a
68 wide range of transformations throughout the hind gastrointestinal tract2,3,7,8. Traditionally, it
69 was believed that BAs undergo amino acid conjugation only in the liver, mediated by a
70 human enzyme known as bile acid-CoA:amino acid N-acyltransferase9,10. However, recent
71 studies demonstrate that in addition to the host, gut microbes can reconjugate BAs via bile
72 salt hydrolase N-acyltransferase activity11,12 . Therefore, hundreds of microbially conjugated
73 amino acid amidates have been identified and termed as novel ‘microbially conjugated bile
74 acids’ (MCBAs)2,3,7,8.
75 BAs are known as emulsifiers, facilitating the absorption of lipids in the gut. They are also
76 important signaling molecules and antimicrobial compound that influence human health. BAs
77 act as ligands to activate various receptors such as farnesoid X receptor (FXR), pregnane X
78 receptor (PXR), Takeda G-protein coupled receptor 5 (TGR5), and sphingosine-1-phosphate
79 receptors (S1PRs)13-15. These receptors regulate cellular growth and differentiation, host
80 energy metabolism, and modulate the glucose and lipid homeostasis. BAs also play a crucial
81 role in the regulation of the immune system by suppressing proinflammatory cytokine
82 production16-18. Dysregulated BA levels are associated with multiple diseases, including type

85 We previously reported that at an early age, systemic BAs including those from the
86 secondary BA pathways are associated with progression to islet autoimmunity and onset of
87 type 1 diabetes (T1D)20. Recently, MCBAs, in particular phenylalanine, tyrosine and leucine
88 conjugated to cholic acid, were also linked with inflammatory bowel disease and cystic
89 fibrosis7,8. However, the early-life dynamics of these MCBAs and their potential role in
90 relation to islet autoimmunity and T1D remain unknown.
91 Herein, in a longitudinal cohort study of young children (ages 3-36 months), we investigate
92 the trajectories of MCBAs during early life including how these microbial BAs are regulated
93 in children who later progressed to islet autoimmunity. The study included children who
94 developed multiple islet autoantibodies (P2Ab) during follow-up and were thus at high risk
95 for progression to T1D later in life21, the children who developed a single islet autoantibody
96 (P1Ab) but did not progress to T1D during follow-up, and the controls (CTRs), i.e., children
97 who remained islet autoantibody (AAb) negative during the follow-up. We also investigate
98 how the MCBAs modulate the innate and adaptive immune responses, and how they
99 associate with profiles of gut bacterial communities in a prospective series of samples.

237 Specifically, we examined the effects of MCBAs during the early in vitro differentiation

250 that MCBAs display specific age-dependent profile during the first three years of life. Certain
251 BA conjugates, specifically primary BA amidates (CA and CDCA), decreased over time,
252 while the secondary BA amidates (DCA and UDCA) increased during early life, before
253 stabilizing. Previous metabolomics studies suggest that age has a significant impact on the
254 longitudinal trajectories of metabolites. Several studies previously found that systemic lipids,
255 including BAs, show distinct age-related trajectories20,24. However, to our knowledge, this is
256 the first study to define the trajectories of MCBAs in the human gut during infancy. Bacteria
257 from the human gut produce bile salt hydrolases (BSHs) that re-amidate BAs to generate
258 MCBAs11,12. During the first three years of life, gut microbial maturation is a dynamic
259 process, which can consequently shape the MCBA profiles and thus explain our observations.
260 The frequencies of MCBA conjugations varied depending on the specific BA type. Notably,
261 the amidates of HDCA and UDCA were the least frequently observed amino acid
262 conjugations. The composition of BAs in humans is markedly influenced by the gut
263 microbiome2,25. Additionally, the human gut microbiome composition differs both within and
264 between individuals26,27, which can influence the amidate conjugations. Recent in vitro
265 studies that investigated bacterial species commonly found in the human intestinal tract, have
266 revealed varying abilities among the gut microbial strains to perform these amidate
267 conjugations5, thus suggesting a complex interplay between microbial diversity and MCBA
268 metabolism within the gastrointestinal ecosystem. Our data also suggest that gut bacteria
269 contribute to the production of these MCBAs.

273 we demonstrated that at-risk children exhibit persistently altered levels of both host-derived

277 BAs are recognized as immunoregulatory metabolites16 and microbially-derived secondary
278 BAs are crucial for the maintenance of immune system homeostasis28. Hang et al. identified
279 that microbially-derived secondary BAs, including 3-oxoLCA and isoallo LCA, affect host
280 immune responses by directly modulating T-cell differentiation16. Moreover, the levels of
281 isoallo LCA were reported to be altered in patients with IBD28. Gentry et al. discovered that
282 some of these newly discovered MCBAs, particularly CA conjugated to Glu, Ile/Leu, Phe,
283 Thr, Trp, or Tyr, could be associated with IBD. In the latter study, for CDCA-Met, DCA-
284 Met, CDCA-Phe, CDCA-Trp, and CDCA-Tyr, a notable increase in the levels of interferon-γ
285 (IFNγ) was reported, with CDCA-Trp showing a sixfold rise8. IFNγ is a cytokine with
286 essential roles in regulating immune homeostasis and inflammatory responses in humans. In
287 line with others, our data suggest that MCBAs control host immune responses by directly
288 influencing in vitro cell differentiation process of Th17 and Treg cells. Given the critical
289 roles of Th17 and Treg cells in inflammatory diseases and their close association with gut
290 bacteria29, our study suggests novel molecular pathways linked to emerging microbiota-
291 derived bioactive compounds that modulate T-cell function.

302 metabolites, which are also known to modulate inflammatory responses and T cell
303 development in human newborns30. Thus, we suggest that disturbances in the gut
304 microbiome-BA axis during early life alters immunomodulation and potentially contribute to
305 the initiation and/or progression of islet autoimmunity.
306 Taken together, our study presents the first known exploration of MCBAs dynamics in early
307 life, revealing their potential role in shaping islet autoimmunity. Our findings show that
308 MCBA levels in stool are closely linked with the gut microbiome and influence the
309 differentiation of Th17 and Treg immune cells, highlighting MCBAs as important modulators
310 of immune development.
